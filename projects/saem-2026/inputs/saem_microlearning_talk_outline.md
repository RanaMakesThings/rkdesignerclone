# SAEM Microlearning Talk Outline

## Working Assumptions

- Audience: emergency medicine educators, clinicians, and education leaders at SAEM
- Length: 12 to 15 minutes
- Goal: make the case that microlearning is rigorous, practical, and especially well suited to emergency medicine
- Tone: evidence-based, modern, pragmatic, not evangelistic

## Core Thesis

Microlearning is not "smaller teaching." In emergency medicine, it is a
workflow-aligned design strategy grounded in learning science that can improve
retention, usability, and uptake when paired with good curation, thoughtful
evaluation, and disciplined use of newer tools such as AI.

## Story Arc

1. The problem: traditional educational formats often do not fit the ED.
2. The science: microlearning works when it uses spacing, retrieval, cognitive load management, and just-in-time relevance.
3. The ecosystem: FOAMed, mobile tools, and asynchronous platforms make delivery practical.
4. The modernization: AI may help personalize and accelerate microlearning, but only with strong guardrails.
5. The action: start small, build deliberately, measure what changes.

## Slide Outline

### Slide 1. Title

- Working title: `Microlearning for Emergency Medicine`
- Subtitle: `Small learning episodes, durable clinical impact`
- Speaker goal: open with the idea that EM has a learning-design problem, not a learner-motivation problem.
- Visual direction: one strong editorial composition suggesting clarity under pressure.

### Slide 2. Why This Matters in the ED

- On-slide message: `Emergency medicine is built around interruption, uncertainty, and time scarcity.`
- Key points:
- Shift work fragments attention.
- Clinical questions arise in real time.
- Traditional long-form education often arrives out of sync with need.
- Speaker emphasis: the problem is structural, not personal failure.
- Evidence anchors: [15], [23]
- Visual direction: timeline or fractured-shift visual showing interruptions and micro-windows.

### Slide 3. What We Mean by Microlearning

- On-slide message: `Brief, focused, high-yield learning episodes designed for action.`
- Key points:
- Usually 5 to 15 minutes
- One concept, case, or skill at a time
- Often mobile, asynchronous, and digitally delivered
- Best when paired with retrieval and repetition
- Speaker emphasis: define it tightly so it does not become "any short thing online."
- Evidence anchors: [1], [13], [27]
- Visual direction: elegant four-part definition grid.

### Slide 4. Why It Works: The Learning Science

- On-slide message: `Microlearning succeeds when it is more than short.`
- Four pillars:
- Spaced repetition
- Active recall
- Cognitive load management
- Just-in-time relevance
- Speaker emphasis: duration alone is not the intervention; design is.
- Evidence anchors: [4], [11], [20], [27], [30]
- Visual direction: four-column framework or stacked pillar system.

### Slide 5. Spaced Repetition + Retrieval in Practice

- On-slide message: `Small repeated retrieval beats one large passive exposure.`
- Key points:
- Daily questions and Anki-style review strengthen retention
- Adaptive review helps target weak material
- Retrieval doubles as both assessment and learning
- Speaker emphasis: this is one of the strongest evidence-backed pieces of the talk.
- Evidence anchors: [11], [12], [20], [28]
- Visual direction: retention pulse or retrieval-wave composition.

### Slide 6. FOAMed as the Delivery Ecosystem

- On-slide message: `FOAMed made microlearning scalable in emergency medicine.`
- Key points:
- Blogs, podcasts, videos, social media, and curated modules
- Rapid dissemination and community-building
- Strong fit for self-directed and point-of-care learning
- Speaker emphasis: FOAMed is a delivery ecosystem, not automatically a quality stamp.
- Evidence anchors: [2], [5], [15], [29]
- Visual direction: ecosystem map instead of a list of logos.

### Slide 7. The Necessary Warning: Not All FOAM Is Signal

- On-slide message: `Accessibility is a strength. Lack of curation is a risk.`
- Key points:
- Quality varies
- Visual polish can falsely signal authority
- Faculty curation and digital literacy still matter
- Speaker emphasis: keep the talk credible by naming the limitations directly.
- Evidence anchors: [5], [15], [29]
- Visual direction: signal-vs-noise comparison with a restrained clinical aesthetic.

### Slide 8. The Tool Stack Already Exists

- On-slide message: `The tool stack already exists.`
- Categories:
- Spaced repetition software like Anki
- Mobile learning apps
- LMS-based asynchronous modules
- Curated FOAMed pathways
- Speaker emphasis: most programs can start with low-cost tools before chasing emerging tech.
- Evidence anchors: [1], [2], [11], [12], [17], [18]
- Visual direction: maturity matrix with `now`, `next`, and `emerging`.

### Slide 9. What AI May Add

- On-slide message: `AI can personalize and accelerate microlearning.`
- Key points:
- Adaptive case selection
- Learner-specific feedback
- Identification of knowledge gaps
- Better timing and sequencing of review
- Speaker emphasis: frame AI as augmentation around good pedagogy, not replacement for pedagogy.
- Evidence anchors: [9], [18]
- Visual direction: adaptive learning pathway, not chatbot clip art.

### Slide 10. What AI Still Cannot Replace

- On-slide message: `AI needs guardrails, curation, and privacy discipline.`
- Key points:
- Faculty oversight remains essential
- Privacy and data handling matter
- Bias, hallucination, and evidence drift are real risks
- Tool novelty should not outrun educational rigor
- Speaker emphasis: this is the credibility slide for the AI section.
- Evidence anchors: [6], [9], [15], [18]
- Visual direction: governance or guardrail frame that still feels modern and calm.

### Slide 11. Design Rules for Good Microlearning

- On-slide message: `Keep it brief, specific, active, and mobile-first.`
- Design rules:
- One learning objective per unit
- Clear immediate relevance to clinical work
- Minimal friction on phones
- Force a response, not just passive viewing
- Use feedback, progress, or light gamification carefully
- Speaker emphasis: if the audience wants a checklist, this is the slide.
- Evidence anchors: [1], [7], [13], [20], [27]
- Visual direction: design checklist or card-based framework.

### Slide 12. Fit It Into Workflow, Not Around Workflow

- On-slide message: `Microlearning works best when it lives where clinicians already are.`
- Practical examples:
- One daily board-style question
- Post-shift clinical pearl
- Curated FOAMed mini-module
- Case-based follow-up after a memorable patient encounter
- Speaker emphasis: show that implementation can be incremental and realistic.
- Evidence anchors: [2], [7], [8], [15], [23]
- Visual direction: ED workflow timeline with learning touchpoints.

### Slide 13. How to Know It Is Working

- On-slide message: `Engagement is not enough.`
- Measures to consider:
- Completion and return rate
- Retrieval performance over time
- Confidence and usability
- Behavior change or workflow uptake
- When feasible, downstream clinical practice measures
- Speaker emphasis: stay honest about the current evidence base and avoid overclaiming patient-outcome effects.
- Evidence anchors: [1], [6], [11], [13], [17]
- Visual direction: measurement ladder from engagement to performance.

### Slide 14. What to Do Monday Morning

- On-slide message: `Start with one pilot, one audience, one metric.`
- Suggested call to action:
- Pick one high-yield topic area
- Build one microlearning unit or daily question series
- Curate rather than create everything from scratch
- Decide in advance how success will be measured
- Speaker emphasis: end with action, not theory.
- Evidence anchors: [2], [8], [15], [23]
- Visual direction: clean three-step implementation roadmap.

### Slide 15. Closing Takeaway

- On-slide message: `In emergency medicine, the best learning design respects how clinicians actually work.`
- Closing line: `Microlearning is not a shortcut. It is a better fit for how modern clinicians learn, remember, and use evidence.`
- Visual direction: return to the opening motif with a more resolved, confident composition.

## Claims to Handle Carefully

- Do not imply that all FOAMed is high quality.
- Do not claim patient-outcome improvement unless you have stronger primary evidence ready for that assertion.
- Do not equate "short" with "effective"; effectiveness comes from design choices.
- Frame AI and XR as emerging or promising, not established standard practice.

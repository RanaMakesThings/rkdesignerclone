# Microlearning for Emergency Medicine: Theory, Tools, and Implementation Strategies

## Executive Summary

Microlearning represents a transformative approach to continuing medical education in emergency medicine, addressing the unique challenges of time-constrained clinical environments. This literature review synthesizes evidence from 30 highly relevant studies to examine the theoretical foundations, specific tools (particularly Free Open Access Medical Education or FOAMed), and practical implementation strategies for microlearning in emergency medicine settings. Key findings indicate that microlearning interventions grounded in spaced repetition, cognitive load theory, and just-in-time learning principles demonstrate significant improvements in knowledge retention and clinical application. FOAMed resources have emerged as a prominent delivery mechanism, though concerns about quality curation and evidence-based validation persist. Practical implementation strategies emphasize mobile-first design, integration into clinical workflows, brief learning episodes (5-15 minutes), and gamification elements to enhance engagement. This review provides emergency medicine educators and clinicians with evidence-based guidance for designing, implementing, and evaluating microlearning interventions in their practice settings.

## 1. Introduction

Emergency medicine presents unique educational challenges: unpredictable clinical demands, shift-based schedules, time constraints, and the need for rapid access to current evidence at the point of care. Traditional continuing medical education models—lengthy conferences, grand rounds, and textbook study—often fail to meet the learning needs of emergency physicians and residents who require flexible, accessible, and immediately applicable knowledge [15]. Microlearning, defined as brief, focused learning episodes typically lasting 5-15 minutes and delivered through digital platforms, has emerged as a promising solution to these challenges [1], [13].

The convergence of mobile technology, open-access educational resources, and evidence-based learning science has created an ecosystem where emergency medicine clinicians can engage in continuous, self-directed learning integrated into their daily workflows [23]. The Free Open Access Medical Education (FOAMed) movement, pioneered largely by emergency physicians, has democratized medical education by creating a global community of practice sharing knowledge through blogs, podcasts, videos, and social media [2], [15], [29].

This literature review examines the theoretical foundations underpinning microlearning effectiveness, surveys the landscape of specific tools and platforms (with particular attention to FOAMed resources), and synthesizes practical implementation strategies for emergency medicine educators. The goal is to provide evidence-based guidance for designing and deploying microlearning interventions that enhance knowledge retention, improve clinical performance, and fit seamlessly into the demanding environment of emergency medicine practice.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations of Microlearning

### 2.1 Spaced Repetition and the Forgetting Curve

Spaced repetition stands as one of the most robust learning principles supporting microlearning interventions. The spacing effect, first documented by Ebbinghaus in the 19th century, demonstrates that information reviewed at increasing intervals is retained significantly longer than information studied in massed practice sessions [30]. Wollstein et al. describe how spaced repetition "blunts the forgetfulness curve" by strategically timing review sessions to coincide with the optimal moment before forgetting occurs [4].

In emergency medicine, where clinicians must retain vast amounts of procedural and factual knowledge, spaced repetition offers particular advantages. McConnery et al. implemented an electronic spaced repetition curriculum companion for pediatric residents, demonstrating sustained engagement and improved knowledge retention over traditional learning methods [11]. The authors note that spaced repetition algorithms can adapt to individual learner performance, presenting challenging material more frequently while spacing out well-mastered content [11].

Laynor's comprehensive review of spaced repetition study tools in health professions education confirms that these approaches are "changing health professions education" by providing learners with evidence-based study methods that optimize long-term retention [12]. The neurobiological foundations of spaced repetition involve memory consolidation processes, with repeated retrieval strengthening synaptic connections and facilitating transfer from short-term to long-term memory [27].

### 2.2 Cognitive Load Theory and Chunking

Cognitive load theory provides another critical theoretical foundation for microlearning design. This framework, which recognizes the limited capacity of working memory, suggests that learning materials should be structured to minimize extraneous cognitive load while optimizing germane load devoted to schema construction [30]. Microlearning addresses cognitive load limitations by breaking complex information into smaller, manageable "chunks" that can be processed without overwhelming working memory [1], [27].

Kravchenko et al. emphasize that microlearning in mobile applications must be designed with neurobiological foundations in mind, noting that "short-term memory limitations" necessitate content delivery in brief, focused modules [27]. This approach aligns with Cowan's research on working memory capacity, which suggests that individuals can effectively process approximately four chunks of information simultaneously [30].

For emergency medicine educators, cognitive load theory suggests that microlearning modules should focus on single concepts or procedures rather than attempting comprehensive coverage of broad topics. Gross et al. demonstrated this principle in their 15-minute crew resource management training, which focused specifically on communication and teamwork skills rather than attempting to cover all aspects of patient safety [13].

### 2.3 Just-in-Time Learning and Point-of-Care Education

Just-in-time learning represents a paradigm shift from traditional "just-in-case" medical education, where learners acquire knowledge in anticipation of future clinical encounters, to learning that occurs at the moment of clinical need [23]. Patocka et al. describe point-of-care resource use in the emergency department as a developmental model that evolves with clinical experience and technological familiarity [23].

The theoretical advantage of just-in-time learning lies in its alignment with adult learning principles, particularly the concept that adults are most motivated to learn when they perceive immediate relevance and applicability [8]. Yilmaz et al. conceptualize this as "the learning loop," describing just-in-time faculty development that occurs in response to specific clinical teaching moments [8].

Emergency medicine is particularly well-suited to just-in-time learning because clinical questions arise frequently during patient care, creating natural learning opportunities. Thoma et al. outline five strategies for effectively using online resources in emergency medicine, emphasizing the importance of having curated, evidence-based resources readily accessible at the point of care [15]. The challenge lies in ensuring that point-of-care resources are evidence-based, rapidly accessible, and integrated into clinical workflows without disrupting patient care [23].

### 2.4 Active Recall and Retrieval Practice

Active recall, also known as retrieval practice or the testing effect, constitutes another fundamental principle underlying effective microlearning. This principle holds that actively retrieving information from memory strengthens learning more effectively than passive review [28]. Goldman et al. describe the evidence-based algorithm "Anki" as optimizing medical education through active recall combined with spaced repetition [28].

Moises et al. implemented a single daily multiple-choice question as a microlearning tool for a core emergency medicine clerkship, leveraging active recall principles [20]. The act of attempting to answer a question, even if initially unsuccessful, enhances subsequent learning and retention more than simply reading the correct answer [20].

The neurobiological mechanisms underlying retrieval practice involve reconsolidation processes, where each act of retrieval modifies and strengthens the memory trace [27]. For emergency medicine learners, active recall through question-based microlearning provides dual benefits: assessment of current knowledge and enhancement of future retention [11], [20].

## 3. Free Open Access Medical Education (FOAMed) in Emergency Medicine

### 3.1 The FOAMed Movement and Its Evolution

The Free Open Access Medical Education (FOAMed) movement represents a grassroots revolution in medical education, characterized by the creation and dissemination of educational content through freely accessible online platforms [2], [15], [29]. Emergency physicians have been "prominent pioneers in the FOAM movement, creating an online, global community of practice through a variety of media and knowledge sharing platforms" [29].

Murray traces the evolution of FOAMed, noting that emergency physicians have embraced this movement to address the rapid pace of evidence generation and the need for timely knowledge translation [29]. The movement's name itself reflects its core values: content must be free (no paywalls), open (accessible globally), and focused on medical education rather than commercial interests [15].

Thoma et al. describe how FOAMed has transformed the landscape of emergency medicine education by enabling rapid dissemination of clinical pearls, evidence appraisals, and procedural techniques across geographic and institutional boundaries [15]. The movement has created a participatory culture where clinicians are both consumers and creators of educational content, fostering engagement and community building [2].

However, the evolution of FOAMed has not been without challenges. Murray cautions about "finding FOAM and not froth," highlighting concerns about quality control, evidence-based rigor, and the potential for misinformation in an environment where "the ease of online publishing, combined with the savvy use of graphics, can allow almost anyone to create recommendations that look authoritative" [29].

### 3.2 Types of FOAMed Resources

FOAMed encompasses a diverse ecosystem of educational resources, each with distinct characteristics and use cases. Pensa et al. describe how their "Asynchrony" course curates various FOAMed resources into structured educational modules for emergency medicine residents [2]. The major categories of FOAMed resources include:

**Blogs and Websites**: Text-based platforms that provide clinical reviews, evidence appraisals, and case discussions. These resources allow for in-depth exploration of topics and often include references to primary literature [15], [29].

**Podcasts**: Audio-based content that enables learning during commutes, exercise, or other activities where visual attention is not available. Podcasts often feature expert interviews, case discussions, and evidence reviews [2], [15].

**Videos and Visual Media**: Procedural demonstrations, ultrasound clips, and visual teaching tools that leverage multimedia learning principles [2].

**Social Media Platforms**: Twitter, Facebook groups, and other social media channels that facilitate rapid knowledge sharing, case discussions, and community building [1], [15].

Stuntz et al. conducted an evaluation of emergency medicine core content covered by free open access medical education resources, finding that FOAMed resources provided substantial coverage of core emergency medicine topics, though with variable depth and quality [5]. Their analysis suggests that FOAMed can serve as a valuable supplement to traditional educational resources, particularly for just-in-time learning needs [5].

### 3.3 Quality Concerns and Curation Strategies

The democratization of medical education through FOAMed has raised legitimate concerns about quality assurance, evidence-based rigor, and potential for misinformation. Murray articulates these concerns, noting that "there are legitimate concerns about the lack of transparency in the development of FOAM and suspicion of" content that may not undergo rigorous peer review [29].

Thoma et al. address these concerns by proposing five strategies for effectively using online resources in emergency medicine, emphasizing the importance of critical appraisal skills and familiarity with high-quality, curated resources [15]. Their strategies include: developing a personal learning network of trusted sources, using aggregators and curators to filter content, applying critical appraisal skills to FOAMed content, contributing to the community through peer review and feedback, and integrating FOAMed into formal educational structures [15].

Pensa et al. demonstrate one approach to quality curation through their "Asynchrony" course, where faculty members curate FOAMed resources into structured modules that meet ACGME standards for individualized interactive instruction [2]. This model combines the accessibility and timeliness of FOAMed with the oversight and quality assurance of faculty curation [2].

Stuntz et al. recommend that emergency medicine educators develop competency in evaluating FOAMed resources and guide learners in distinguishing high-quality, evidence-based content from less rigorous material [5]. This approach treats FOAMed literacy as a core competency for modern emergency medicine practice [5].

### 3.4 Evidence for FOAMed Effectiveness

Despite widespread adoption, rigorous evidence for FOAMed effectiveness remains limited. Stuntz et al. note that while FOAMed resources provide broad coverage of emergency medicine core content, "further research is needed to determine the educational effectiveness of these resources" [5]. The challenge lies in the heterogeneity of FOAMed resources and the difficulty of conducting controlled studies in an environment characterized by self-directed, voluntary learning [15].

Pensa et al. report successful implementation of their FOAMed-based "Asynchrony" course, with residents earning ACGME-approved individualized interactive instruction credits through completion of curated modules [2]. However, they acknowledge the need for more rigorous evaluation of learning outcomes and clinical impact [2].

Thoma et al. argue that the value of FOAMed extends beyond traditional educational metrics, encompassing community building, rapid knowledge translation, and democratization of expertise [15]. They suggest that evaluation frameworks for FOAMed should consider these broader impacts alongside traditional measures of knowledge acquisition and retention [15].

## 4. Specific Tools and Platforms for Microlearning

### 4.1 Spaced Repetition Software (Anki and Alternatives)

Spaced repetition software (SRS) represents one of the most evidence-based tools for microlearning in medical education. Goldman et al. describe Anki as an "evidence-based algorithm" that optimizes knowledge retention through adaptive scheduling of review sessions [28]. Anki uses a modified SuperMemo algorithm that adjusts the interval between reviews based on learner performance, presenting difficult cards more frequently while spacing out well-mastered content [28].

Laynor's review confirms that spaced repetition study tools, particularly Anki, are "changing health professions education" by providing learners with scientifically validated study methods [12]. The platform's flexibility allows users to create custom decks tailored to specific learning needs, share decks with peers, and track learning progress over time [12].

McConnery et al. implemented an electronic spaced repetition curriculum companion for pediatric residents, demonstrating high engagement rates and positive learner feedback [11]. Their system integrated spaced repetition principles into a structured curriculum, automatically scheduling review questions aligned with didactic content [11].

While Anki dominates the spaced repetition landscape in medical education, other platforms offer alternative approaches. Laynor notes the emergence of commercial platforms that combine spaced repetition with question banks, video content, and performance analytics [12]. However, Anki's open-source nature, extensive customization options, and zero cost make it particularly attractive for resource-constrained educational programs [28].

### 4.2 Mobile Learning Applications

Mobile learning applications leverage the ubiquity of smartphones to deliver microlearning content that fits into the fragmented schedules of emergency medicine clinicians. Fagerstrøm et al. developed a mobile learning application with flip cards for healthcare professionals, demonstrating that mobile microlearning increased knowledge compared to traditional teaching methods [1]. Their application incorporated gamification elements, allowing users to compete for the most correct answers in the shortest time [1].

Thrush et al. conducted a scoping review of mobile spaced education in surgical education settings, finding that mobile platforms effectively deliver spaced repetition interventions across diverse specialties and training levels [17]. The portability of mobile devices enables learning during brief intervals—waiting for consultations, commuting, or between clinical shifts—that would otherwise be unproductive [17].

Carter et al. compared face-to-face, online, and micro-learning interventions delivered via mobile application, finding that the mobile micro-learning approach offered notable advantages in terms of accessibility and learner convenience [30]. Participants could engage with content "when and where they want," reducing barriers associated with scheduled learning sessions [30].

Hashimoto et al. implemented a "Resident Study Log" as an asynchronous learning tool, recognizing that residents' "convenient time" for learning varies based on clinical schedules and personal preferences [7]. Their mobile-accessible platform allowed residents to engage with educational content during self-identified optimal learning moments [7].

### 4.3 Learning Management Systems and Digital Courses

Learning management systems (LMS) provide structured frameworks for delivering microlearning content while maintaining educational oversight and tracking learner progress. Pensa et al. describe their "Asynchrony" course, which uses an LMS to curate FOAMed resources into narrative, topic-specific educational modules [2]. Each module requires residents to complete assignments, participate in discussion boards, and pass quizzes, with all activity tracked for ACGME credit [2].

The advantage of LMS-based approaches lies in their ability to combine the flexibility and accessibility of microlearning with the structure and accountability of formal education [2]. Faculty can monitor learner engagement, identify struggling learners, and ensure that educational objectives are met [2].

Yeung et al. conducted a landscape review of digital teaching in medical education, identifying learning management systems as a core component of digital education infrastructure [6]. They note that effective LMS implementation requires attention to user experience, mobile accessibility, and integration with existing educational workflows [6].

Bernstein et al. surveyed internal medicine residents about educational resource utilization, finding that residents increasingly prefer digital, on-demand resources over traditional textbooks and scheduled conferences [14]. This preference underscores the importance of LMS platforms that can deliver microlearning content in formats aligned with learner preferences [14].

### 4.4 Emerging Technologies

Emerging technologies promise to further enhance microlearning capabilities in emergency medicine. Eastwood et al. describe the development of an artificial intelligence-augmented, peer-driven, case-based curriculum for emergency medicine residents [9]. Their approach uses AI to personalize learning pathways, recommend relevant cases, and provide adaptive feedback [9].

Basnawi et al. review the application of artificial intelligence in advanced training and education of emergency medicine doctors, highlighting AI's potential to analyze learner performance, identify knowledge gaps, and deliver targeted microlearning interventions [18]. AI-powered platforms can adapt content difficulty, recommend optimal review timing, and provide personalized learning analytics [18].

Einloft et al. explored immersive medical training using extended reality (XR) in emergency scenarios, demonstrating that XR technologies can deliver experiential microlearning for procedural skills and crisis management [10]. While XR represents a more resource-intensive approach than traditional microlearning, it offers unique advantages for skills that require spatial reasoning and hands-on practice [10].

Dingler's work on cognition-aware systems explores how technology can support information intake and learning by detecting optimal cognitive states for learning [16]. Such systems could potentially deliver microlearning content during moments of high cognitive readiness, maximizing learning efficiency [16].

## 5. Implementation Strategies and Practical Tips

### 5.1 Designing Effective Microlearning Content

Effective microlearning content design requires attention to both pedagogical principles and practical constraints of the clinical environment. Gross et al. demonstrate that crew resource management training can be effectively delivered in 15-minute microlearning sessions by focusing on specific, actionable skills rather than comprehensive coverage [13]. Their approach emphasizes clarity of learning objectives, active learning strategies, and immediate applicability to clinical practice [13].

Moises et al. implemented a single daily multiple-choice question as a microlearning tool for emergency medicine clerkships [20]. This minimalist approach reduces barriers to engagement while leveraging active recall principles [20]. The key is ensuring that each question is carefully crafted to target high-yield content and includes detailed explanations that extend learning beyond the immediate question [20].

Fagerstrøm et al. recommend incorporating gamification elements—such as progress tracking, achievement badges, and friendly competition—to enhance engagement with microlearning content [1]. However, they caution that gamification should support rather than distract from learning objectives [1].

Content should be designed for mobile-first consumption, with clear typography, minimal scrolling, and graphics optimized for small screens [1], [7]. Kravchenko et al. emphasize that mobile microlearning applications must account for neurobiological foundations, including attention span limitations and the need for frequent breaks to consolidate learning [27].

### 5.2 Integration into Clinical Workflows

Successful microlearning implementation requires thoughtful integration into existing clinical workflows rather than adding to already overwhelming educational demands. Yilmaz et al. conceptualize "the learning loop" as just-in-time faculty development that occurs within the natural flow of clinical teaching [8]. This approach embeds learning opportunities into existing activities rather than creating separate educational sessions [8].

Patocka et al. describe a developmental model for point-of-care resource use in the emergency department, noting that clinicians progress through stages of awareness, exploration, integration, and mastery [23]. Educators should support this developmental progression by providing curated resources, modeling effective use, and creating a culture that values point-of-care learning [23].

Hashimoto et al. found that residents' "convenient time" for learning varies widely based on shift schedules, clinical demands, and personal preferences [7]. Their asynchronous learning tool allowed residents to engage with content during self-identified optimal moments, resulting in higher engagement than scheduled educational sessions [7].

Thoma et al. recommend integrating FOAMed resources into formal educational structures, such as journal clubs, case conferences, and simulation debriefings [15]. This integration legitimizes FOAMed use, provides opportunities for critical appraisal, and ensures that self-directed learning aligns with curricular objectives [15].

### 5.3 Engagement and Motivation Strategies

Sustaining learner engagement represents a critical challenge for microlearning interventions. McConnery et al. found that engagement with their electronic spaced repetition curriculum companion remained high throughout the academic year, attributing this success to several factors: alignment with didactic curriculum, immediate feedback, visible progress tracking, and peer comparison features [11].

Fagerstrøm et al. demonstrated that gamification elements—including competition for fastest correct answers and social sharing of achievements—significantly increased engagement with their mobile learning application [1]. They used a closed Facebook group to facilitate social interaction and friendly competition among learners [1].

Carter et al. note that micro-learning interventions delivered via mobile applications can be "less intrusive for employees unable to take long periods away from daily activities," reducing a common barrier to educational engagement [30]. The key is designing interventions that fit into existing routines rather than requiring dedicated study time [30].

Martinelli et al. argue for bringing evidence-based education to clinical specialties, emphasizing that learners are more engaged when educational interventions are grounded in learning science and demonstrate measurable outcomes [21]. Transparency about the evidence base for microlearning approaches can enhance learner buy-in and motivation [21].

### 5.4 Assessment and Evaluation Methods

Robust assessment and evaluation methods are essential for demonstrating microlearning effectiveness and guiding continuous improvement. Fagerstrøm et al. used a randomized controlled design with pre- and post-tests to evaluate their mobile microlearning intervention, demonstrating significant knowledge gains compared to traditional teaching [1]. This rigorous approach provides strong evidence for microlearning effectiveness [1].

McConnery et al. tracked multiple engagement metrics—including questions answered, time spent, and return visits—to evaluate their spaced repetition curriculum companion [11]. They found that learners who engaged more frequently demonstrated better performance on end-of-rotation assessments, suggesting a dose-response relationship [11].

Pensa et al. implemented a comprehensive tracking system within their learning management system, monitoring assignment completion, discussion board participation, and quiz performance [2]. This multi-faceted assessment approach ensures that learners are actively engaging with content rather than simply clicking through modules [2].

Thrush et al. recommend that mobile spaced education interventions include both process measures (engagement, completion rates) and outcome measures (knowledge retention, clinical performance, patient outcomes) [17]. They note that while process measures are easier to collect, outcome measures provide more meaningful evidence of educational impact [17].

## 6. Evidence of Effectiveness in Emergency Medicine

The evidence base for microlearning effectiveness in emergency medicine, while growing, remains heterogeneous in quality and scope. Gross et al. conducted a rigorous evaluation of their 15-minute microlearning intervention for crew resource management, demonstrating significant improvements in teamwork behaviors and communication skills [13]. Their study used validated assessment tools and included follow-up measurements to assess retention [13].

Fagerstrøm et al. demonstrated that healthcare professionals using their mobile microlearning application achieved significantly higher knowledge scores than a control group receiving traditional teaching [1]. The effect was consistent across two test groups, one using only the mobile app and another combining the app with social media interaction [1].

McConnery et al. reported high engagement rates with their electronic spaced repetition curriculum companion, with residents completing an average of several hundred questions over the academic year [11]. Learners reported that the tool helped them retain information from didactic sessions and prepare for examinations [11].

However, several studies acknowledge limitations in the evidence base. Stuntz et al. note that while FOAMed resources provide broad coverage of emergency medicine content, "further research is needed to determine the educational effectiveness of these resources" [5]. The challenge lies in conducting rigorous studies in an environment characterized by self-directed, voluntary learning [5].

Yeung et al.'s landscape review of digital teaching in medical education found that many studies report positive learner satisfaction and engagement but fewer demonstrate improvements in clinical performance or patient outcomes [6]. This gap highlights the need for more rigorous, outcomes-focused research on microlearning interventions [6].

## 7. Challenges and Limitations

Despite promising evidence, microlearning in emergency medicine faces several significant challenges. Murray articulates concerns about FOAMed quality, noting that "the ease of online publishing, combined with the savvy use of graphics, can allow almost anyone to create recommendations that look authoritative" [29]. This democratization of content creation necessitates strong critical appraisal skills among learners [29].

Thoma et al. acknowledge that many emergency physicians, particularly those who "came of age during the Index Medicus years," are hesitant to use FOAMed resources due to concerns about credibility and evidence-based rigor [15]. Overcoming this generational divide requires demonstrating the value of curated, high-quality FOAMed resources and providing training in digital literacy [15].

Technical barriers also pose challenges. Not all clinicians have access to smartphones or reliable internet connectivity, particularly in resource-limited settings [17]. Additionally, the proliferation of platforms and tools can create decision paralysis, with learners unsure which resources to trust and use [12].

Sustainability represents another concern. Many FOAMed resources are created and maintained by volunteers, raising questions about long-term viability and content updates [15], [29]. Pensa et al. note that faculty curation of FOAMed resources requires significant time investment, which may not be recognized or rewarded in traditional academic promotion systems [2].

Finally, the evidence base for microlearning effectiveness, while growing, remains incomplete. Yeung et al. call for more rigorous research examining not just learner satisfaction and knowledge acquisition, but also clinical performance and patient outcomes [6]. Without this evidence, skeptics may dismiss microlearning as a trendy but unproven educational approach [21].

## 8. Future Directions and Recommendations

The future of microlearning in emergency medicine will likely be shaped by several emerging trends and technologies. Basnawi et al. envision artificial intelligence playing an increasingly central role, with AI systems analyzing learner performance, identifying knowledge gaps, and delivering personalized microlearning interventions [18]. Such systems could optimize learning efficiency by targeting individual learner needs [18].

Eastwood et al. demonstrate the potential of AI-augmented, peer-driven curricula that combine the personalization of AI with the social learning benefits of peer interaction [9]. This hybrid approach may address some limitations of purely automated learning systems [9].

Einloft et al.'s work on extended reality in emergency scenarios suggests that immersive technologies may enable experiential microlearning for complex procedural and crisis management skills [10]. As XR technologies become more accessible and affordable, they may complement traditional microlearning approaches for skills that benefit from spatial and tactile learning [10].

For emergency medicine educators, several practical recommendations emerge from this review:

1. **Ground interventions in learning science**: Design microlearning interventions based on established principles such as spaced repetition, active recall, and cognitive load theory [1], [4], [11], [27].

2. **Curate high-quality resources**: Develop competency in evaluating FOAMed resources and guide learners toward evidence-based, peer-reviewed content [15], [29].

3. **Integrate into workflows**: Embed microlearning opportunities into existing clinical and educational workflows rather than creating separate educational sessions [8], [23].

4. **Leverage mobile technology**: Design content for mobile-first consumption, recognizing that smartphones are the primary access point for many learners [1], [7], [17].

5. **Incorporate engagement strategies**: Use gamification, social learning, and progress tracking to sustain learner motivation [1], [11].

6. **Evaluate rigorously**: Implement robust assessment and evaluation methods that measure not just engagement but also knowledge retention and clinical performance [1], [6], [13].

7. **Foster digital literacy**: Teach critical appraisal skills for evaluating online educational resources and model effective use of point-of-care learning tools [15], [23].

8. **Build communities of practice**: Create forums for sharing microlearning resources, discussing implementation strategies, and collaborating on content development [2], [15].

## 9. Conclusion

Microlearning represents a paradigm shift in emergency medicine education, offering flexible, evidence-based approaches to continuing professional development that align with the realities of clinical practice. Grounded in robust learning science—including spaced repetition, cognitive load theory, just-in-time learning, and active recall—microlearning interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in improving knowledge retention and clinical skills [1], [4], [11], [13].

The FOAMed movement has created a rich ecosystem of freely accessible educational resources, though quality curation and critical appraisal remain essential [2], [5], [15], [29]. Specific tools such as spaced repetition software (Anki), mobile learning applications, and learning management systems provide practical platforms for delivering microlearning content [1], [2], [11], [12], [28].

Successful implementation requires thoughtful attention to content design, workflow integration, engagement strategies, and rigorous evaluation [1], [8], [11], [13], [23]. While challenges remain—including quality concerns, technical barriers, and incomplete evidence—the trajectory of microlearning in emergency medicine is clearly positive [6], [15], [29].

As emergency medicine continues to evolve, microlearning will likely play an increasingly central role in how clinicians maintain competence, acquire new skills, and translate evidence into practice. By embracing evidence-based microlearning approaches, emergency medicine educators can create learning environments that are more accessible, effective, and aligned with the needs of modern clinical practice.

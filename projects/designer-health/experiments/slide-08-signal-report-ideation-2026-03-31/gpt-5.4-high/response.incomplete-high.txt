Here’s the real ask:

## 1) What the user is actually trying to get onto the slide

They are **not** trying to show “the app.”

They are trying to show **one believable clinician-facing artifact** that proves the product can take a messy patient conversation and turn it into a **structured brief a doctor could actually use**.

The slide needs the viewer to instantly get:

- this is **not raw transcription**
- this is **not just a chatbot**
- this is **not a dashboard of random widgets**
- this is a **clinical brief** that captures:
  - the story
  - the key findings
  - the likely considerations
  - what still needs confirmation
  - the next best question

If the reaction isn’t “oh, the clinician gets *this*,” then the slide misses.

---

## 2) My blunt design opinion on what the artifact should be

The artifact should be a **single report-like sheet**, not a product screenshot and not a split-screen dashboard.

That means:

- **one central white brief**
- **minimal chrome**
- **clinical headings**
- **tight information hierarchy**
- **small, restrained reasoning area on the right**
- **a bottom timeline strip**
- **one explicit unresolved/questions area**

### What it should not be

- not a browser window
- not a fake EMR with nav/sidebar/tabs
- not a dense dashboard
- not a giant “Differential Diagnosis” panel screaming “we diagnose patients now”
- not covered in loud green checks and red Xs like a consumer app

That last part matters. The user likes the **supports / against / unknowns logic**, and that logic is good. But the presentation should be **calm and clinician-ready**, not gamified.

### My strong opinion

If you copy the reference component too literally, you will end up with a **nice-looking ops console**, which is the wrong vibe for this slide.

The winning move is:

> **A structured brief document with embedded reasoning, not a reasoning UI with some patient facts attached.**

That’s the whole game.

---

## 3) Three concrete composition directions, ranked best to worst

# 1. Best: Single structured brief with embedded reasoning rail

### One-sentence visual description
A single clean white “Intake Brief” sheet, with the left two-thirds showing the distilled patient story and findings, the right third showing ranked working considerations, and a full-width bottom timeline anchoring the story in time.

### What to keep from the reference component
- the calm white clinical-card feel
- thin borders
- muted typography
- ranked candidates
- the **Supports / Against / Unknowns** logic
- a suggested next question

### What to cut from the reference component
- trace/provenance tabs
- raw ops-console feel
- visible tab systems
- too many separate cards
- anything that reads like internal tooling

### What to translate or rename
- `What We Know` → break into actual clinical sections:
  - `Chief concern`
  - `Why today`
  - `Key positives`
  - `Key negatives`
  - `What happened when`
- `Differential + Next Question + Trace` → split into:
  - `Working considerations`
  - `What still needs confirmation`
  - `Suggested next question`
- `Unknowns` → `To confirm` or `Still unclear`

### Why it works on an investor deck
Because it shows the **transformation**:
conversation in → structured clinical brief out.

That is the product. Not the UI chrome. Not the trace. Not the AI guts.

It also matches the official slide intent almost perfectly:
- **what matters** → key positives / negatives
- **what happened when** → timeline
- **what still needs confirmation** → unresolved items + next question

### Biggest risk / failure mode
If you overpack it, it becomes unreadable and the slide turns into tiny text soup.

This direction only works if you’re ruthless:
- 6–8 sections max
- very short bullets
- only 3 considerations
- only 1 next question
- timeline with 4 nodes, not 10

---

# 2. Good but weaker: Two-panel artifact derived from the reference component

### One-sentence visual description
A single framed artifact containing two side-by-side cards: left for patient story and extracted findings, right for working considerations with supports/against/to-confirm logic.

### What to keep from the reference component
- the two-column balance
- the ranked list format
- supports/against/unknowns structure
- the next-question behavior
- restrained status color

### What to cut from the reference component
- trace
- raw provenance
- any tabs
- extra controls
- “sandbox” flavor

### What to translate or rename
- left card `What We Know` → `Patient story + key findings` or just `Key findings`
- right card `Differential + Next Question + Trace` → `Working considerations`
- `Unknowns` → `To confirm`
- add a small timeline row below the left card

### Why it works on an investor deck
It’s fast to understand. It clearly says:
- left = extracted clinical picture
- right = reasoning + next question

And it preserves the sharpness the user liked in the reference.

### Biggest risk / failure mode
It reads like a **dashboard**.

That is the problem. Even if it’s visually attractive, it drifts away from “structured brief” and toward “clinical decision-support tool UI.” On this slide, that’s weaker.

---

# 3. Worst: Browser-framed webpage screenshot of the mock report

### One-sentence visual description
A faux browser window with the report inside it, showing the brief as if the clinician opened a webpage.

### What to keep from the reference component
- overall visual calm
- report card styling
- clean section hierarchy
- the reasoning logic

### What to cut from the reference component
- tabs
- trace
- raw console elements
- any visible app scaffolding beyond the report itself

### What to translate or rename
Mostly the same clinical renaming as above, but housed inside a browser frame.

### Why it works on an investor deck
It instantly signals “this is software,” which is the only reason people keep reaching for this move.

### Biggest risk / failure mode
It’s the wrong move.

It shrinks the content, adds useless chrome, and turns the slide into a screenshot instead of an artifact. The eye goes to the browser frame instead of the brief. It also violates the stated guidance: **one central artifact, not a screenshot, not a dashboard**.

If you do this, the slide gets less believable, not more.

## 4) Should the right-hand reasoning area be labeled “Differential,” “Working differential,” “Working considerations,” “Clinical considerations,” “Considerations to confirm,” or something else?

Use:

# **Working considerations**

That is the correct label.

### Why not the others

- **Differential**  
  Too aggressive. Too loaded. Feels like you’re claiming diagnostic authority on the slide.

- **Working differential**  
  Better, but still very “we are producing the diagnosis list.” It invites the wrong read.

- **Clinical considerations**  
  Too vague. Sounds like consultant mush.

- **Considerations to confirm**  
  Good phrase, but not for the main reasoning area. That should be a **separate box for unresolved items**, not the title of the ranked list.

### My blunt answer
Show the reasoning logic, yes.  
Do **not** make the headline word on the slide be **Differential**.

If you absolutely must preserve the concept, do it inside the panel structure, not in the panel title.

Example:
- panel title: `Working considerations`
- within each item:
  - `Supports`
  - `Against`
  - `To confirm`

That gives you the same intelligence without the wrong posture.

## 7) A few very specific visual rules I’d enforce

Because this kind of mock lives or dies on restraint.

### Typography
- section labels: small, muted, semi-bold
- content: dark gray, not pure black
- candidate titles: slightly heavier, not oversized
- no giant title inside the artifact

### Color
- mostly grayscale
- supports: muted green dot or left border
- against: muted rust/red dot
- to confirm: amber or warm gray
- no candy-color chips

### Icons
Do **not** use giant checkmarks and Xs everywhere.

Use:
- tiny dots
- small pills
- maybe a subtle arrow in the timeline

The minute it looks like a consumer health app, the slide gets worse.

### Density
- 3 working considerations max
- 3–4 bullets in key positives
- 0–1 bullets in key negatives if sparse
- 3 unresolved items max
- 1 next question only

## 8) Blunt recommendation: if I had to ship one believable mock for the deck today, what exactly would I build first?

I would build **Direction 1** immediately:

> **A static HTML “Intake brief” artifact, exported as an image, with a left clinical-summary column, a right “Working considerations” rail, and a full-width bottom timeline.**

### Exactly what I’d put in it

#### Header
- `Intake brief`
- tiny tag: `For clinician review`

#### Left side
- `Chief concern`  
  `Cough and breathing changes`

- `Why today`  
  `Cough for about one week after URI symptoms, with yellow sputum and measured fever that has not clearly resolved.`

- `Key positives`
  - `URI prodrome present`
  - `Cough for about one week`
  - `Yellow sputum present`
  - `Measured fever present`

- `Key negatives`
  - `None confidently established yet`

#### Right side
- `Working considerations`
  1. `Community-acquired pneumonia`
     - Supports: `yellow sputum`, `fever unresolved`
     - Against: `no strong confirmed counter-evidence yet`
     - To confirm: `dyspnea severity`, `pleuritic pain`

  2. `Acute bronchitis`
     - Supports: `URI prodrome`, `cough duration`
     - Against: `fever persistence makes simple bronchitis less clean`
     - To confirm: `breathing impact`

  3. `Post-viral cough`
     - Supports: `preceding URI`
     - Against: `productive sputum and fever are less typical`
     - To confirm: `fever resolution`

- `What still needs confirmation`
  - `Has breathing changed from baseline?`
  - `Any chest pain with deep breath?`
  - `Is the fever ongoing or gone?`

- `Suggested next question`
  - `How has your breathing changed, and does it hurt anywhere in the chest when you take a deep breath?`

#### Bottom
- `What happened when`
  - `URI symptoms begin`
  - `Cough develops (~1 week ago)`
  - `Yellow sputum noted`
  - `Measured fever still unresolved today`

### Then I would do one more thing
I would place **three zoom callouts on the slide** pointing to:
- `Key positives` → **what matters**
- `What happened when` → **what happened when**
- `What still needs confirmation` → **what still needs confirmation**

That’s the version I’d ship first.

Not a dashboard.  
Not a screenshot.  
Not a fake full product.

A single, sharp, clinician-ready brief. That’s the believable mock.

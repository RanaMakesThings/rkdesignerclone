It needs to be realistic enough that an investor looks at it and immediately understands: *this is the deliverable the doctor receives, and it's obviously better than a clipboard of scribbled chief complaints.* It is not a product screenshot of a dashboard. # My blunt design opinion

The reference component the user likes (the Designer ops sandbox) is too operations-console. It's two cards side by side with tabs, trace panels, raw provenance—it looks like something an engineer built to debug a reasoning chain, not something a physician would glance at between patients. The *logic* inside it is excellent (supports/against/unknowns is genuinely sharp), but the *container* is wrong for a pitch deck and honestly wrong for a clinician audience too.

What the artifact should feel like is a **single-page clinical summary document**—closer to a well-designed discharge summary or a pre-visit brief in Epic's sidebar than to a two-pane developer tool. Think: one unified card with clear vertical sections, not a left-right split dashboard. The reason is simple: clinicians scan top to bottom, not left to right. They are trained on documents, not dashboards. And on a pitch deck, a single cohesive artifact reads as "this is a thing we make" whereas two side-by-side cards reads as "this is a UI we're still figuring out."

The differential / working considerations section should absolutely be present, but it should feel like a subsection of the brief, not a co-equal panel. The brief is the product. The differential is one section of the brief. Hierarchy matters.

I also think the user is right that a timeline or structured extraction on the left/top is critical. Investors need to see: the AI didn't just transcribe—it organized, it reasoned, it flagged gaps. The timeline-to-differential flow is the whole story: *we captured this, we reasoned about it, and here's what's still uncertain.* That narrative arc is the product.

# Should the right-hand reasoning area be labeled "Differential"?

**No.** Not in the deck.

Here's my reasoning:

- `Differential` is a loaded clinical term. It implies the AI is diagnosing. Investors will either (a) not know what it means, or (b) know exactly what it means and immediately start thinking about liability, FDA, and scope of practice. Either way, it's a distraction on a pitch slide.
- `Differential diagnosis` is even worse. You're not making a diagnosis. You're structuring the intake to surface what the clinician should be thinking about.
- `Working differential` is better but still carries the "diagnosis" baggage.
- `Considerations to confirm` is too passive. It sounds like a to-do list, not a reasoning output.
- `Clinical considerations` is fine but generic. It doesn't communicate that the AI actually *reasoned* about these—it sounds like a static list.

**My recommendation: `Working Considerations`**

It communicates:
1. These are active, not final ("working")
2. They are things to consider, not diagnoses ("considerations")
3. The AI did reasoning work to produce them

It's honest about what the product does without overstepping into diagnostic territory. In the actual product later, you might use `Working Differential` for clinician audiences who want clinical language. But in a pitch deck seen by mixed audiences, `Working Considerations` is the right call.

If you want to be slightly bolder and the audience skews clinical: `Working Assessment` also works. But `Working Considerations` is safer and equally clear.

# Blunt recommendation

**If I had to ship one believable mock for the deck today, I would build Direction 1 as a single self-contained HTML file.**

Specifically:

- One `<div>` styled as a white card with a subtle `box-shadow` and `border-radius: 8px`, max-width around 640px, on a neutral (#f5f5f7) background.
- Four semantic sections inside it: header, key findings, working considerations, flagged for confirmation.
- Use exactly the cough case content above. Don't abstract it—real content is what makes a mock believable.
- Status badges as small `<span>` pills: green for Present, amber for Unresolved. No red—nothing is "wrong," things are just unresolved. Red implies error or danger; amber implies "needs attention," which is the correct clinical framing.
- Supports/Against/Unknowns as inline text with a small colored arrow or icon prefix. Green `↑` for supports, muted gray `↓` for against, amber `?` for unknowns. Restrained. Not a Christmas tree.
- The whole thing should render in a browser, screenshot cleanly, and drop into a slide as a PNG or embedded image.
- Total build time if you know what you're doing: 90 minutes for the HTML/CSS, 30 minutes to fuss with the slide placement and callout overlays.

Do not build the two-column version. Do not build a dashboard. Do not add tabs. Do not add interactivity. This is a picture of a clinical document that an AI produced. That's the product. Make it look like something a doctor would trust, not something a developer would admire.

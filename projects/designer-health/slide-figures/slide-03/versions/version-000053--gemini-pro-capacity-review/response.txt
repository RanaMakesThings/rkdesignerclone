This is a classic visual communication challenge: translating a mathematical concept (time saved $\times$ volume $=$ new capacity) into an intuitive graphic without triggering negative assumptions (rushing patients). 

The core problem with the current slide is **ambiguity of scale and state**. Viewers don't know if the middle row is the "before" and the bottom row is the "after," or if they are just two different visual representations of the same idea. Furthermore, the visual math doesn't add up intuitively—the eye cannot easily verify that the slivers of time saved in the blue blocks equal the size of the green block.

Here are five concrete redesign directions, followed by reactions to your specific ideas, and a final recommendation.

---

### 1. Five Redesign Directions

#### Direction 1: The Fixed Timeline (Before & After)
*   **The Visual Change:** Create a literal horizontal axis representing a fixed block of time (e.g., a 4-hour half-shift). 
    *   Row 1 ("Current"): Show 5 full-length visits filling the timeline. 
    *   Row 2 ("With AI"): Show 6 shorter visits filling the *exact same* timeline length. Ensure the gray blocks (exam/counseling) are visually identical in size to Row 1, and only the blue blocks are shorter.
*   **Why it clarifies:** It immediately solves the "where did the 6th visit come from?" problem. By fixing the total width of the row, the viewer instantly understands that the total time worked hasn't changed, but throughput has.
*   **Tradeoff/Risk:** It can look a bit like a Gantt chart, which might feel slightly more operational or dry than a conceptual illustration.

#### Direction 2: The "Banked Time" Accumulation
*   **The Visual Change:** Keep a "Before" row of 5 visits. Below it, show the "After" row. In the "After" row, shrink the blue section, but leave a dashed, empty outline where the rest of the blue *used* to be. Draw subtle arrows from those 5 empty dashed boxes pooling together to form the solid green "New Visit" at the end.
*   **Why it clarifies:** It makes the math explicit. It visually answers the question: "How does saving a few minutes create a whole visit?" by showing the fractions adding up to a whole.
*   **Tradeoff/Risk:** It introduces more visual elements (dashed boxes, arrows) which could make the slide feel cluttered or overly complex if not designed with a very light touch.

#### Direction 3: The "Protecting the Core" Emphasis
*   **The Visual Change:** Exaggerate the visual distinction between the variable time (blue) and the protected time (gray). Make the gray blocks a more solid, grounded color. Add a bracket over the gray sections labeled "Unchanged: High-quality face-to-face care." Only show the blue section shrinking. 
*   **Why it clarifies:** This directly addresses your constraint. It visually proves that you are not compressing the physical exam or the human connection (counseling). You are only compressing the administrative/reconstruction burden.
*   **Tradeoff/Risk:** It focuses heavily on the *quality* constraint, potentially at the expense of making the *capacity* math (5 becomes 6) immediately obvious.

#### Direction 4: The Micro-to-Macro Progression
*   **The Visual Change:** Delete the middle row entirely. 
    *   Step 1 (Top): Show one large "Current Visit" bar. Below it, show one "New Visit" bar, with an arrow pointing inward to show the blue section shrinking.
    *   Step 2 (Bottom): Show a simple equation or visual: [Icon of 5 old bars] $\rightarrow$ [Icon of 6 new bars].
*   **Why it clarifies:** It reduces cognitive load by separating the concepts. First, understand *how* a single visit changes. Second, understand the *result* of that change at scale.
*   **Tradeoff/Risk:** You lose the visual representation of the "compounding" effect happening in real-time across a shift.

#### Direction 5: The Vertical Stack (Calendar View)
*   **The Visual Change:** Instead of horizontal bars, stack the visits vertically, like blocks in a calendar app (e.g., Google Calendar). Show a "Current Schedule" column with 5 blocks filling the day. Next to it, show a "New Schedule" column with 6 slightly shorter blocks filling the same day.
*   **Why it clarifies:** Doctors and clinic managers think in terms of vertical daily schedules. This maps the abstract concept directly onto a UI they look at every day.
*   **Tradeoff/Risk:** It requires a complete layout change from the current horizontal orientation, which might not fit the visual style of the rest of your deck.

### 2. Reactions to Specific Ideas

*   **Two new visits instead of one:** **Strongly advise against.** If you claim two new visits, you violate your core constraint. It will immediately trigger skepticism. To fit two extra visits into a standard shift, you would have to slash the visit time drastically, which reads as reckless compression and lower quality. Stick to one; it's credible and still represents a massive revenue/capacity increase (a 20% bump).
*   **A key or legend:** **Avoid.** Legends force the viewer's eyes to dart back and forth between the key and the graphic, increasing cognitive load. Direct labeling (as you have on the top bar) is much better.
*   **Arrows showing the blue segment shrinking:** **Yes.** This is a great micro-interaction to add. A simple set of inward-pointing arrows on the blue segment of a "before/after" single visit makes the mechanism of action instantly clear.
*   **An efficiency or capacity arrow:** **Yes, if used to show accumulation.** An arrow sweeping from the saved time across the 5 visits and pointing to the 6th green visit helps tell the story of "compounding."
*   **Changing the middle transition text:** **Highly recommended.** "Efficiency repeated across visits creates capacity" is passive and abstract. Use concrete numbers. Example: *"Saving just 4 minutes of prep per visit creates time for 1 additional patient per shift."*
*   **Making the before/after transformation more explicit:** **Mandatory.** The current slide fails primarily because the viewer doesn't know what the middle row represents compared to the bottom row. You must clearly label "Current State" and "Future State."

---

### 3. Preferred Recommendation

I recommend a hybrid of **Direction 1 (Fixed Timeline)** and **Direction 3 (Protecting the Core)**. 

Here is exactly how to execute it:

1.  **Keep the top macro bar.** It's good. But add a visual cue (like inward arrows) to the blue section to show it is the target for reduction.
2.  **Delete the current middle row.** It is confusing.
3.  **Create a clear "Before & After" comparison.** 
    *   Label the first row **"Current Schedule (5 visits)"**. Show 5 bars.
    *   Label the second row **"AI-Assisted Schedule (6 visits)"**. Show 6 bars.
4.  **Fix the width.** Both rows must start and end at the exact same horizontal points on the slide. This visually communicates "same length of shift."
5.  **Protect the gray.** Ensure the light gray and dark gray sections in the "AI-Assisted" row are the *exact same width* as they are in the "Current" row. Only the blue section should be narrower. 
6.  **Update the transition text.** Place it between the two rows: *"Saving just a few minutes of story reconstruction per patient compounds to allow one extra visit—without shortening the physical exam or counseling."*
7.  **Keep the 6th visit green.** The color pop is good, but now that the timeline is fixed, the viewer will understand *why* it fits.
